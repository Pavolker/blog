# The Cultural Stock

My difficulty begins with the expression "genuinely human work." What would, after all, a human work be that did not depend on everything that came before it? I do not write alone. No one writes alone. I write in a language I did not invent, with words I received, images I inherited, ways of narrating that were already in the world before me. I write from books read, conversations, films, music, memories, experiences, schools, cities, friends, losses, desires, and an entire culture that precedes me. The author is not the origin of the cultural stock. He is a point of passage.

Every culture has a stock: language, symbolic repertoire, historical memory, techniques, institutions, artistic media, ways of living together, knowledge, beliefs, instruments, and forms of expression. This stock delimits the space in which a society can create. To delimit is not to determine. The combinations can be nearly inexhaustible. But no one invents from nothing the elements with which they combine. An eighteenth-century European composer had at hand certain instruments, a harmonic tradition, a notation system, certain musical forms, and the soundscape of his time. An Ancient Greek sculptor could not make cinema. Not because he lacked talent, but because cinema was not yet part of the stock of possibilities of his culture.

All creation is an operation upon antecedents. It can combine, invert, condense, expand, displace, transform, negate. Sometimes it reorganizes what came before so profoundly that it expands the cultural stock itself: it introduces a new technique, a language, a form of perception. But even in that case it did not arise "ex nihilo." It transformed something that already existed. For humans there will never be the "ex nihilo," simply because, for any human, at birth, existence is given "ab omni" — already with everything. To be human is to be born into a chain that is already underway.

It is to receive a culture that does not belong to us individually and to return to it new configurations. The subject of the stock is not the isolated author. It is humanity in history. That is why the contemporary obsession with absolute originality seems strange to me. No one goes through a scientific article, sentence by sentence, trying to find out whether each formulation has already appeared at some point in history. Citations are not proof of absolute originality; they are a limited way of recognizing relevant antecedents. Bach's work is not worth less because it belongs to a tradition. It can only be understood because it belongs to a tradition.
