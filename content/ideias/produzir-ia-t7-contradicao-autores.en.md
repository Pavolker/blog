# The Contradiction of the Authors

It is precisely here that I find the most uncomfortable contradiction. Authors and musicians — people who know, better than anyone, that every work is born from a collective heritage — rise up against a human work, mediated by AI, because it threatens a certain image of the author: that of the individual, autonomous, originary creator, separated from the stock that made him possible. They denounce AI as an intruder. But the intruder did not come from outside. It was built by us, with our materials, from our history.

Concern about transparency can be legitimate. A person may wish to know whether they are listening to a singer who exists, who has lived certain experiences and who interprets a song with her own voice. Another may be interested only in the music, the lyrics, the harmony, the arrangement, or the aesthetic effect. These are different relationships with a work. Informing about the mode of production can be important. But transparency does not require inventing an ontological frontier between the human and what humanity has built.

It is also legitimate to discuss compensation. Who should be recognized and paid when a system reorganizes a heritage formed by millions of contributions? Who controls the infrastructure? Who decides access to the models? Who profits when a platform distributes, recommends, and monetizes this content? These questions are decisive. But they belong to the governance of the cultural stock, not to a war between humanity and machine.

Yuval Noah Harari calls AI "alien intelligence." He clarifies that he is not speaking of something from space, but of an intelligence that makes decisions differently from human beings. Still, the metaphor produces a false exteriority. A difference in mechanism does not turn a technique into something foreign to the culture that created it. Writing is not orality. Photography is not painting. The synthesizer is not the violin. But none of these instruments came from outside humanity.

Miguel Nicolelis, when he asserts that AI is "neither intelligent nor artificial," makes the opposite move. His critique can be useful for refusing to attribute consciousness or biological intelligence to computational systems. But the cultural question does not depend on resolving whether a model feels or not. The decisive fact is that it materially participates in the reorganization of human culture and produces real effects within it.
