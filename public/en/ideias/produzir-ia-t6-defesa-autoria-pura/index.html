Defending Pure Authorship Is Disloyalty to History

Defending Pure Authorship Is Disloyalty to History

Defending pure authorship is a disloyalty to history. It is a disloyalty because it rests upon a fiction: that the author creates alone, from within himself, something that did not belong to anyone before. The history of culture refutes this fiction on every page. And AI, by performing recombination at an unprecedented scale, is not introducing something new. It is merely making visible what has always been before us: the collective, ancestral, imitative, technical, and accumulative dimension of what we have always called creation.

To say that a piece of music was “made by AI” is, in this sense, a technical reductionism, not a sufficient conceptual description. It may indicate that the immediate generation was executed by a model. But it becomes misleading when it suggests that AI created something by itself, as an originating subject external to humanity. There is no AI process today that does not have, at minimum, a human origin: someone conceived the architecture, established objectives, chose data, built infrastructure, financed the project, configured the system, gave an order, or instituted the process that will trigger it. Even when the generation seems automatic, there is a prior human decision that created the conditions for automation.

AI is not a rival culture. It is a human technique for reorganizing the human stock. This technique is already altering cultural production. In music, Deezer reported that it went from approximately ten thousand fully AI-generated tracks received per day in January 2025 to about ninety thousand at the peak of June 2026 — more than half of the platform’s daily new uploads. In publishing, studies on independent fiction on Amazon record an explosive catalog growth in the period following the spread of generative models. These numbers are often presented as proof of an invasion. I read them differently: they show that humanity has found a new instrument for reorganizing its cultural heritage at an unprecedented scale.

And this instrument does more than increase the volume of content. It reduces barriers. People who did not know how to play instruments, record, produce, edit, translate, lay out, illustrate, or structure a text can now transform an idea into a publishable song or book. Self-publishing already existed, digital distribution already existed. But AI makes viable, for far more people, the passage between having something to say and being able to give public form to that something.
