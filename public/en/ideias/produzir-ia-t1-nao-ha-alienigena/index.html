There Is No Alien in the Human Cultural Cauldron

AI as a human work, not as a foreign invasion. First text of the 'Producing AI' family.

There Is No Alien in the Human Cultural Cauldron

I am an author. I have been writing systematically since I was fifteen. Perhaps that is why the way many professional authors and musicians have reacted to artificial intelligence in culture bothers me so much. It doesn’t bother me that they defend fair compensation, credit, transparency, or working conditions. Those are necessary issues. What bothers me is something else: the idea that books, music, and images produced with AI would be a foreign invasion, a kind of non-human force that arrived to corrupt a hitherto genuinely human culture.

AI may be, perhaps, one of the most human works we have ever produced. Not because it is a person, nor because it feels, suffers, or has a biography. But because it is capable of speaking, with knowledge of cause and fact, about an immense expanse of what humanity has recorded about itself. It is made of human language, human memory, human science, human art, human technique, human institutions, human errors, human imagination. And, more than that, it opens the possibility for a much larger portion of humanity to access this heritage and work upon it.

But they claim that something “made by AI” is not human. There is something profoundly mistaken in this position. It presupposes that an ontological frontier exists between the human and what humanity has built. As if an instrument could cease to be human simply because it is powerful. The axe does not cease to be human because it cuts more than the hand. The telescope does not cease to be human because it sees farther than the eye. AI does not cease to be human because it reorganizes more culture than an isolated individual could.

The resistance to AI in culture reveals, at bottom, a certain image of what it means to be human: the image of the solitary, self-sufficient creator who pulls works out of himself like drawing water from an original spring. This image has always been a fiction. The difference is that now we have an instrument that makes this fiction untenable. And that is why the reaction is so intense. It is not only about defending a market or working conditions. It is about defending an identity.

What is at stake is not whether AI can or cannot create. What is at stake is whether we accept that creation has always been, and continues to be, a collective, ancestral, technical, and accumulative process. Whether we accept that the author is not the origin of the cultural stock, but a point of passage. Whether we accept that there is no alien in the human cultural cauldron — only humans doing what they have always done: reorganizing the heritage they received.
